Front Crawl Troubleshooting Guide

Fixing sinking legs, mouthfuls of water, and early exhaustion — and why all three are actually the same problem.

The One Problem 1 · Sinking Legs 2 · Breathing 3 · Fatigue Practice Plan Checklist

Your three issues are one issue

It's worth seeing this before drilling into each problem, because it tells you where to spend your effort.

Here's the chain:

  1. Your legs sink → your body is tilted diagonally in the water, like a plow.
  2. A plow has enormous drag → you need much more power just to move, so your arms and legs work overtime → fatigue.
  3. A tilted, struggling body has no stable platform to breathe from → your mouth is lower in the water, the bow wave pocket never forms properly, you rush and gulp → mouthfuls of water.
  4. Bad breathing means oxygen debt → you tense up and kick harder to compensate → tense, heavy legs sink even more → back to step 1, worse.
What this means for practice: Body position is upstream of everything. Most of your fatigue is not weak muscles — it's the cost of dragging a diagonal body through water plus the panic of oxygen debt. Fix the balance first, then breathing becomes mechanically possible, then fatigue largely solves itself. Resist the urge to "train harder" — train flatter.

Issue 1: Why your legs sink even with a level head

You've correctly learned that looking down keeps the spine aligned and that lifting the chin sinks the legs. But head position is necessary, not sufficient. Here's the missing physics.

Your lungs are a buoy. Your legs are an anchor.

Your chest contains several liters of air — it floats powerfully. Your legs are dense muscle and bone — they sink, in basically everyone (lean men especially). Your body is a seesaw pivoting around your lungs:

This is the classic "press the buoy" concept (from Total Immersion, but every school teaches some version). If you float passively with a level head and do nothing else, your legs will still sink — that's just your density distribution. You have to actively lean on your chest: a constant, gentle downward pressure on your sternum, as if someone's hand were pushing between your shoulder blades. Because water pushes back on your buoyant chest, the seesaw tips and your hips and legs ride up.

The feeling to chase: swimming slightly "downhill." It feels wrong at first — like you'll dive to the bottom. You won't; your lungs won't let you. When you get it, your hips suddenly feel light and the backs of your legs break the surface. Cue: swim downhill or lean on your lungs.

The other three leg-sinkers to check

CauseWhat's going onFix / cue
Soft core If your midsection is relaxed, your body bends at the waist like a banana and the legs droop, regardless of head position. Lightly brace your abs and glutes the whole time — about 30% effort, like a gentle plank. Cue: long and firm, like a kayak, not a banana.
Floppy or hooked ankles Kicking with flexed feet (toes toward your shin) is like kicking with anchors on — it pushes water forward and drags your legs down. Very common in adult learners, especially runners and cyclists with stiff ankles. Point your toes (plantar-flex) so the top of your foot pushes water backward. Floppy, loose ankles — let the water flick the foot. Cue: ballerina toes. Short fins for drills are a legitimate learning aid here.
Kicking from the knee A knee-driven "bicycle" kick drops the thighs deep and creates drag instead of lift. You know to kick from the hip — but check that the knees stay nearly straight with only a soft, whip-like bend. Small, fast, narrow kicks that stay inside the "shadow" of your body. Splashing the surface lightly with your heels is a good sign. Cue: kick from the hip, boil the water, don't splash the ceiling.
Important reframe: the kick's job at your stage is not propulsion — it's to keep the legs up and balance the stroke. A light, steady flutter is enough if you're pressing your chest. If you're kicking furiously to keep your legs afloat, you're using the kick to fight a balance problem, and that's exactly what's exhausting you (see Issue 3).

Balance drills (do these before anything else)

Drill A — Superman glide

Goal: feel what a balanced, level body actually feels like, with zero stroke to distract you.

Push off the wall underwater, arms extended, face down, eyes at the pool bottom, body firm. No kicking, no stroking. Glide and notice when your legs start to drop. Then repeat, this time pressing your chest down as you glide. You should feel your hips stay up noticeably longer. 8–10 repeats. This drill is boring and it is the highest-value thing you can do right now.

Drill B — Glide + gentle kick

Goal: add the kick without losing the balance.

Same as Drill A, but after the glide slows, start a small, soft flutter kick from the hips, toes pointed. Keep pressing the chest. Stop the moment you feel your hips drop, stand up, reset. Quality over distance — 15 meters done level beats 50 meters done diagonal.

Drill C — Kick on your side

Goal: balance in the rotated position you'll actually breathe from.

One arm extended forward (underwater, fingertips slightly below the wrist), the other arm resting on your hip. Rotate your body ~45° toward the resting-arm side, face looking down, gentle flutter kick. When you need air, rotate your head (not your body) to breathe, then return your face to the water. This is the skeleton of the freestyle breathing position. Fins allowed and encouraged at first.

Issue 2: Breathing — and what that YouTube comment actually means

You already have the most important piece: exhale fully underwater so the breath window is inhale-only. Keep doing that — a steady trickle out of the nose/mouth the whole time your face is down, finishing with a stronger puff just as you turn. If you're holding your breath at all, the CO₂ buildup alone will make you feel desperate by cycle 4 — which matches your symptom exactly.

So why the mouthfuls of water? Two reasons: where the air actually is, and how you're aiming your mouth at it.

The bow wave and the trough

As you move forward, your head pushes water aside like the bow of a boat. This creates a bow wave in front of and beside your head — and immediately behind that wave, next to your cheek and jaw, the water surface dips below the normal waterline. That dip is the trough, the famous "air pocket." It means you can inhale with your mouth lower than the actual surface of the pool — but only if your mouth opens into the trough, and only if you're moving forward with a level body (no forward motion = no bow wave = no pocket; sinking legs = head plowing low = pocket drowned).

Decoding the comment: "turn your chin to your shoulder"

Here is what that commenter figured out, translated.

Most learners, when they "rotate to breathe," do this: they rotate the body, then tilt the face straight up toward the ceiling, so the mouth points vertically at the sky — perpendicular to the body, as the commenter puts it. Two bad things happen:

  • Tilting the face up is a disguised version of lifting the head — it pulls the head out of alignment with the spine, which (as you know) drops your legs at the worst possible moment.
  • A sky-facing mouth sits at the top of the head's rotation, right where the bow wave crests and washes over. You're aiming your mouth at the wave, not the trough.

The fix: instead of tilting the face upward, rotate the head around the spine's axis and tuck the chin slightly toward the shoulder of the breathing side (the side whose arm is recovering over the water). Now the mouth points sideways-and-slightly-back, at roughly 45° toward your shoulder rather than straight up. That orientation drops your mouth directly into the trough behind the bow wave — the spot beside your cheek where the water has dipped away.

The whole comment in one sentence: the air pocket is not above your face, it's beside and slightly behind it — so don't point your mouth at the sky (90° to your body); rotate your head so the chin moves toward your shoulder and the mouth aims ~45° back toward it. And the commenter's caveat matters: this only works after the body position is fixed, because the trough only exists when you're moving level through the water.

How to know you're doing it right

  • One goggle stays in the water. If both goggles clear the surface, you've over-rotated or lifted. You should see half sky, half water — "split vision."
  • You're looking sideways/slightly behind you, roughly toward your armpit or the lane rope — not at the ceiling, not toward where you're going.
  • Your head turns with your body's roll, then a touch more. The body rotation (which you do on every stroke anyway) does 80% of the work; the chin-to-shoulder turn is the last 20%. Breathing is a head rotation, never a head lift.
  • The breath is quick. Mouth opens in the trough, one sharp inhale ("sip the air, don't gulp it"), face back down before the recovering arm's hand enters the water.

Breathing drills

Drill D — Sink-down bobs (do these in the shallow end, even before swimming)

Goal: make full exhalation automatic and kill breath-holding panic.

Stand in chest-deep water. Inhale through your mouth, sink/squat under, and exhale a slow, continuous stream of bubbles until your lungs are genuinely empty — it takes longer than you think, 4–6 seconds. Surface, one quick inhale, repeat. Do 20. The rhythm you're building — long relaxed exhale, instant snap inhale — is exactly the swimming rhythm.

Drill E — Side-kick with chin-to-shoulder breath

Goal: rehearse the exact head movement in isolation.

Drill C position (one arm forward, on your side at ~45°, face down, gentle kick). Exhale steadily. When empty, rotate only your head, chin toward the top shoulder, until your mouth clears into air — one goggle still wet — inhale, return face down. Stay in this position for a full pool length, breathing every 3–4 seconds. Fins make this drill dramatically easier to learn; use them.

Drill F — Whole-stroke, breathe every 2, exhale loudly

Goal: integrate it into real swimming with maximum air supply.

Swim normal freestyle but breathe every 2 strokes (every cycle, same side) — forget "every 3 / alternate sides" for now; that's a styling refinement for later, and right now it's just starving you of air. Hum out loud underwater so you can hear that your exhale is continuous. Swim 15–25m, rest fully, repeat. Stop each rep before your form falls apart.

Issue 3: Why you're exhausted (it's not fitness)

A fit person who runs or bikes can still be wrecked by 25 meters of freestyle. That's normal, and it's diagnostic: your exhaustion is an efficiency problem, not a conditioning problem. Three leaks, in order of size:

Leak 1: Drag from the diagonal body (the big one)

Drag rises roughly with the square of speed and balloons with frontal area. A swimmer with dragging legs presents several times the frontal area of a level swimmer. You are likely spending well over half your energy just paying the drag tax. Every balance fix from Issue 1 is therefore also a fatigue fix — this is why the guide keeps pointing you back there.

Leak 2: The survival kick

Kicking is the most oxygen-hungry thing you can do in the water — the legs are huge muscles working far from the lungs, and the flutter kick contributes maybe 10% of propulsion for most adult swimmers. If you're kicking hard to hold your sinking legs up, you're burning your biggest fuel reserve on a job that chest-pressing does for free. Once balance improves, deliberately downshift to a light, narrow, almost lazy flutter — just enough to keep the legs in your body's shadow. Your heart rate will drop immediately.

Leak 3: Arm spin without traction

You know the phrase "high elbow," so here's just the practical version of it. The point of the high elbow is to make your forearm + palm into one big paddle that pushes water backward:

  • After your hand enters and extends, tip your fingertips down and keep your elbow high and wide, near the surface — like reaching over a barrel or a fallen log in front of you.
  • Then press the whole forearm-paddle back toward your feet, past your hip. Water pushed backward moves you forward; water pushed downward only lifts your front and sinks your legs (another hidden leg-sinker!).
  • A straight-arm pull that slips downward feels powerful but moves mostly water, not you — that's why your arms burn without much to show for it.
Slow down to speed up. The single best anti-fatigue instruction: take fewer, longer, more patient strokes. Glide a beat with each extended arm. Count your strokes per length and try to lower the count, not raise the turnover. Rushed strokes slip; patient strokes grip.

Effort-management rules for your current stage

  • Swim short and rest honestly. 15–25m repeats with 30–60s rest, focusing on one cue per repeat. Continuous laps with collapsing form just rehearse the collapse.
  • One cue at a time. "Press chest" for one length. "Ballerina toes" the next. "Chin to shoulder" the next. The brain can't hold five cues mid-swim.
  • Stay loose. Tension is dense and sinks; tension also burns oxygen. Soft hands, relaxed recovery arm (the elbow leads, the forearm dangles), loose ankles.
  • Stop each repeat while form is still good. That's the rep doing its job.

A practice progression

Each session ~30–45 minutes. Move to the next stage when the current one feels easy, not after a fixed number of visits. Fins are a learning tool, not cheating — they let you hold good positions long enough for your nervous system to memorize them; wean off them gradually.

Poolside quick-reference

Body (fixes the legs)

  • Eyes down, neck long — crown of the head leads
  • Lean on your chest — swim "downhill," hips light
  • Core gently braced — kayak, not banana
  • Toes pointed, ankles floppy, kick small and from the hip

Breath (fixes the water-swallowing)

  • Trickle-exhale the entire time your face is down; strong puff at the end
  • Rotate the head with the body roll — never lift it
  • Chin toward the shoulder, mouth ~45° back — into the trough, not at the sky
  • One goggle stays wet; quick sip, face straight back down
  • Lead arm stays extended out front while you breathe
  • Breathe every 2 strokes for now

Effort (fixes the exhaustion)

  • Fewer, longer, patient strokes — count them per length
  • High, wide elbow; press water backward with the forearm, not downward
  • Downshift the kick to "lazy" once your balance holds
  • Short repeats, real rest, one cue at a time, stop while form is good
